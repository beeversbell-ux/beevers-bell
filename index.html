HOW THE “YOUR CITY, YOUR VOICE” CONSULTATION UNFOLDED

Prepared as a resident-led community record for Kingsville, this site follows two proposals that became the focus of sustained local concern: a permanent public toilet at Beevers Reserve and the partial closure of Coronation Street beside Bell Reserve.

THE ISSUES RESIDENTS RAISED
Child + pedestrian safetyincluding children using the parks and surrounding streets
Traffic redistributiononto nearby residential streets if Coronation Street is closed
Emergency + service accessincluding how larger vehicles move through the local street network
Visibility + natural surveillanceand the effect of a permanent toilet on Beevers’ open sightlines
Antisocial-behaviour riska recurring concern recorded in the consultation feedback
Whether Beevers fits Council’s toilet frameworkfor the provision and siting of a permanent public toilet

The wider park upgrades were broadly supported. This record focuses on the two contested elements, what the consultation actually recorded, what Council endorsed in April, and what happened when residents later took the evidence, process and safety questions back to Council.

Council’s own toilet framework

Does Beevers meet Council’s own test for a permanent public toilet?

Resident account of the consultation“PEER SURVEILLANCE”

Residents who attended the consultation say a local Wattle Ward councillor suggested “peer surveillance” when antisocial-behaviour concerns were raised. Residents understood this as surrounding homes providing informal observation of the park. The attribution is presented as a resident account of the session.

COUNCIL’S PUBLIC TOILET PLAN

Regional and municipal parks are prioritised over neighbourhood and local parks.

Council’s Public Toilet Plan focuses new provision on popular public spaces that support extended stays, including key regional and municipal open spaces. In a 2024 Council meeting, the CEO said the toilet program is based on the park hierarchy and that regional and municipal parks are given priority over neighbourhood and local parks.

Beevers Reserve was not one of the ten locations listed for new toilets in the 2019 plan.

AND IF A TOILET IS PROVIDED

Council also sets clear location and safety criteria.

VISIBLEWELL-LITCLEAR SIGHTLINESOPEN ENTRANCES

Council says Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design principles are “essential” for future facilities. Its April report says visible, well-lit locations with clear sightlines support natural surveillance and discourage antisocial behaviour, while clear, open entrances improve access control.

Council’s current project FAQ adds that, if a toilet is installed, it would be in a visible, high-traffic area with clear sightlines and good lighting, with entry and exit points oriented toward active public areas.

COUNCIL’S OWN DESCRIPTION OF BEEVERS

“Currently a local park size.”

Council’s April report describes Beevers as currently a local park size. Council’s case is that planned park improvements, including the shelter and BBQ, are expected to increase visitation and support longer stays.

Those park improvements are not the issue here. The unresolved question is whether Beevers itself has been demonstrated to satisfy the Public Toilet Plan’s provision and location framework.

Look at the setting: the photographs on this page show the park’s existing open layout and homes directly opposite the reserve.

RESIDENT CONCERN

The issue is bigger than which part of the park a toilet could occupy.

Residents have raised whether a permanent toilet belongs in this small residential reserve at all, given Council’s own park-hierarchy, visibility, surveillance and neighbour-impact criteria.

THE QUESTION THAT REMAINS

Has Beevers been shown to satisfy Council’s Public Toilet Plan?

Council’s framework raises two separate questions: whether Beevers is the kind of public space prioritised for toilet provision, and whether a toilet can be located here while meeting Council’s visibility, lighting, sightline and natural-surveillance principles.

19 MAY 2026PUBLIC QUESTION TIME

Council’s Director of Infrastructure Services said the relevant safety and design principles would be addressed through detailed design, including layout, location and lighting.

This does not establish that the toilet is unsafe. It shows that those considerations were still to be worked through after concept endorsement.

WHAT THE RECORD SHOWS

Council endorsed the concept on 21 April. Council’s 22 April update also says the toilet will be reviewed under the Public Toilet Plan and, if progressed, installed through the public toilet delivery program rather than as part of the park upgrades.

The public record presented here does not establish that Beevers has already completed that assessment.

Two different terms: “peer surveillance” is a phrase residents say was used during consultation. “Natural surveillance” is the CPTED term in Council’s formal report. They are not presented here as interchangeable.

THE SAME NIGHT · 16 JUNE

The questions were opening up. The project did not pause.

Council’s 22 April update had said further site investigations and other works at Beevers and Bell were set to begin in the next financial year, subject to the 2026/27 Budget. On 16 June, Council adopted that Budget. Its published budget summary included $150,000 for the Bell Reserve upgrade.

That makes June a second hinge in the chronology: the concepts remained endorsed, the petition was formally received, councillors responded to questions about what had been understood before the April vote, and the Budget condition identified by Council in April was passed that same evening.

This did not remove the separate statutory process still required for the Coronation Street closure, or the separate Public Toilet Plan assessment for the Beevers toilet.

THE CORONATION STREET RECORD
Traffic redistribution · safety · evidence

Closing Coronation Street moves traffic elsewhere. What was tested?

Council’s traffic assessment concluded that the surrounding road network could accommodate the predicted redistributed traffic. Residents then asked what that redistribution meant at street level for pedestrians, children and emergency access.

WHAT THE REPORT ANSWERSCan the surrounding road network accommodate the predicted redistributed traffic?
WHAT RESIDENTS WERE ALSO ASKINGWhat would those redistributed movements mean for children, pedestrians and emergency access on the surrounding streets?
WHY THAT DIFFERENCE MATTERS

Showing that surrounding streets can carry the extra traffic is not the same as showing what that traffic means for children, pedestrians or emergency access. The report supports a conclusion about whether surrounding streets can absorb the predicted traffic. This review did not locate a dedicated assessment connecting that extra traffic to child and pedestrian exposure around Wales Street Playground, or a quantified route-specific emergency-response analysis.

Closing part of Coronation Street would not make the existing traffic disappear.

The assessment predicts that some vehicles would instead use nearby residential streets. It then estimates how much extra traffic those streets would receive.

02 · STREET-LEVEL CONTEXT

The resident concern is not only whether the network can absorb the traffic, but what redistributed movements mean at street level for pedestrians and children.

03 · WHAT THE REPORT TESTED

What the report tested

Traffic volumes, redistribution, intersection operation and delay, crash history, pedestrian continuity, emergency access and service vehicles. Its detailed quantified analysis is principally about traffic operations.

What this review did not find in the report

A dedicated child or pedestrian-safety assessment connecting the predicted redistribution to exposure and crossing behaviour around the nearby playground, or route-specific emergency response-time testing.

The remaining evidence question: what establishes that the changed layout is acceptable for pedestrians, children and emergency access, beyond network capacity?

This does not establish that the closure is unsafe. It identifies the narrower safety issues residents raised and the limits of what the original operational assessment establishes.
